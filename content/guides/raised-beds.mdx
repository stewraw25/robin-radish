A raised bed is a frame full of soil. It gives you a clear edge, a path you can walk on, and a way to grow on ground that is compacted, stony, or paved. It is not magic soil. Weeds, slugs and watering are still yours to do.

If you are still choosing where the vegetables will go, start with [starting a vegetable patch](/guides/starting-a-veg-patch). A bed on open ground, with no frame, grows the same crops. If you would rather not turn the soil, see [no-dig beds](/guides/no-dig-beds). Spacing for each crop is on its own page, for example [carrots](/grow/carrots) and [kale](/grow/kale).

## Size

Make the bed narrow enough to reach the middle without standing on the soil. If you can walk both sides, about 1.2 metres is a comfortable width for most people. If one side is against a fence, keep it nearer 60 to 75 centimetres.

Length can follow the garden. Height of 15 to 30 centimetres is enough for many crops when the base is open onto decent ground, because roots use the soil underneath. Go deeper if you are on rubble or paving, if bending is difficult, or if you want carrots and parsnips to live entirely in the filled soil.

Leave paths wide enough for you. If you use a wheelbarrow, make one path wide enough for it. Grass paths creep into the bed unless you edge them. Woodchip, gravel or paving stay put.

## Materials

Untreated durable timber, such as larch or cedar, or heat-treated timber sold for beds, is a common frame. It will not last forever in wet UK weather. That is fine if you can repair a corner later.

Do not build a food bed from old railway sleepers that may have been treated with creosote. Do not use painted or preserved timber if you do not know what is in the coating. Bricks, blocks and slabs are fine if they are stable and will not leach anything you would mind in a vegetable bed. Avoid tyres for food crops.

On soil, leave the base open so water can drain and worms can move. Cardboard over lawn, under the new soil, rots away and suppresses the grass for a while. On paving, you need a base that holds the soil in and still lets water out. Drilling or leaving gaps is part of the build. A sealed box becomes a swamp.

## What to fill it with

Use a mix of topsoil and garden compost or a bought soil improver. A bed filled only with compost settles, shrinks and dries out. You do not need peat. Leaf mould and garden compost are enough organic matter for most vegetables.

Fill in layers and walk the paths, not the bed, as you go. The surface will sink in the first year. Top it up the next spring. Do not fill above the frame and expect it to stay there after rain.

## Water and the first crops

Raised beds drain faster than flat ground. In a dry June they need water sooner. A mulch on the surface, once seedlings are up, slows that. They can also be wetter than you think in winter if the mix is heavy and the base cannot drain. If water sits on the surface for days, the drainage is wrong.

Almost anything in the [sowing planner](/planner) can grow in a bed of this sort. Root crops need the depth. Mint still belongs in a pot even if the bed is large, because it will take the whole frame. Courgettes need the sun and a fair amount of room. Salad, beans, chard and herbs are a sensible first summer.

## Common questions

### Can I put a raised bed on concrete?

Yes, if water can leave the base and the bed is deep enough for the crops. It will dry out faster and it will be heavier than it looks, so build it where it will stay. Do not block a damp-proof course or a drain.

### How long does a wooden bed last?

It depends on the timber, the wet, and whether the joints stay sound. Several years is a fair hope for durable timber, not a promise. A rotten corner can be replaced without throwing the soil away.

### Should the bed be lined with plastic?

A plastic liner that seals the bottom holds water. That is the wrong problem to solve. If you line the sides to keep wet soil off the timber, leave the base free to drain. Cardboard under a bed on lawn is temporary and does not need to be plastic.
