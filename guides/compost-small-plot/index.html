Compost for a small plot

By Robin & Radish · 17 November 2026

How to make compost in a small UK garden, what to leave out of the heap, and how to use it on a vegetable bed.

A small garden can still make compost. One bin, or a heap a metre across, is enough. You will not produce trailer-loads. You will produce a dark mulch for the vegetable bed, which is the point.

Spread finished compost on the surface. You do not have to dig it in. That use is described in no-dig beds. A framed bed and its filling are in raised beds.

What goes in

Mix soft green material with dry brown material. Greens are vegetable peelings, coffee grounds, annual weeds that have not set seed, and spent lettuce plants. Browns are torn cardboard, screwed-up paper, dead leaves and the woody stems of a finished bean row once they are chopped small.

Keep the heap as mixed as you can. A thick layer of wet peelings on its own turns slimy. A thick layer of cardboard on its own just sits there. Alternate them.

A lid or a square of carpet keeps a winter heap from becoming a pond. In a UK winter the heap slows down. That is normal. It wakes up in the warmer months. Turning it with a fork speeds it up. If you never turn it, it still rots. It takes longer, often many months rather than one season. There is no fixed week when it is ready.

It is ready when you cannot tell what it was, and it smells earthy rather than sour. The bottom of an unturned bin is often ready while the top is still peelings. Use the finished part and put the rest back.

What stays out

Do not add cooked food, meat, fish, dairy or oily scraps. They attract rats. Do not add cat litter or dog waste.

Leave out the roots of bindweed, couch and other perennial weeds, and weeds that have set seed, unless you are sure the heap gets hot enough to kill them. Most small bins do not. Perennial roots grow again when you spread the compost.

Do not compost brassica roots if you know clubroot is in the garden, or onion waste if you have had white rot. You would be spreading the problem. Diseased tomato and potato foliage is better in the council green waste, if they take it, than on a small cool heap.

Plastic, glossy coated card and compostable-looking bags that do not actually break down should stay out. Tear tape off cardboard.

On the bed

A layer of a few centimetres on the soil in autumn or winter is a useful mulch. Keep it off the stems of rosemary, thyme and other plants that rot at a wet neck. Do not sow carrots and parsnips into compost that is still full of lumps and fresh kitchen bits. Let it finish, or use a finer layer. The carrot and parsnip pages explain why fresh manure and rough patches fork the roots. Compost is not a pesticide, and it does not replace rotation.

Bins and bags

A heap on the soil works. If you want a container, you can search Amazon for a compost bin (opens in a new tab), or for garden compost (opens in a new tab) if you are buying a bag to spread while your own heap finishes. Those are searches, not a particular bin or bag.

Common questions

Can I compost in a small plastic bin?

Yes. Drill holes if the bin is a sealed tub with no air, and stand it on soil if you can so worms can move in. A sealed, airless bucket of peelings is not compost. It is a smell.

Do I need a compost activator?

No. A mix of green and brown material, some moisture, and time is the method. A spadeful of finished compost or garden soil adds the organisms. A bought activator is optional, not required.

The heap smells sour. What went wrong?

It is too wet, or too full of soft scraps and not enough cardboard and stems. Add torn card, fork it so air gets in, and keep the rain off. A sour heap is not ready to spread on a salad bed.
