First frost dates in a UK garden

By Robin & Radish · 24 November 2026

How to judge first and last frost in a UK garden, and how to protect plants, without treating a published date as a promise.

A first frost date is the first night in autumn when the air at screen height, or the ground in your own garden, drops to 0°C or below. A last frost date is the last such night in spring. Both move from year to year, and they move from one garden to the next. This page does not give a single date for your postcode, and it does not invent a table of town averages.

The Met Office publishes forecasts and climate information for the UK. Use a forecast for the week ahead, and your own notes, rather than a printed date from a book. A max-min thermometer in the garden, out of the rain and away from a warm wall, is more useful than a guess.

If you garden in Ireland, start with the Ireland sowing page. That page uses this site's UK north list as the cautious start, and it points to Met Éireann and Teagasc. This frost note is written in the UK voice.

Why two gardens differ

Coastal, low, and built-up gardens are often milder than inland, high, or open ones. A garden on the south or west coast can go well into autumn before a frost, and can finish frosts earlier in spring. A valley, a hollow, or a north-facing plot can be a frost pocket, even when a hill a mile away stays clear. A city terrace is often a little warmer than a field at the same latitude.

The south and north ranges on this site already allow for that spread. South, on the sowing planner, means southern England, the Midlands and much of Wales. North means northern England and Scotland. Those are ranges of months, not a forecast for one street. A wet, cold spring can hold either region back. So can a late April frost.

Do not move every date by a fixed number of weeks because a neighbour had a frost last Tuesday. Write down the nights that actually frost in your own garden, over a few winters if you can. That list is the one that matters.

What to protect, and what to leave

Tender plants — tomatoes, courgettes, cucumbers, sweetcorn, French beans, basil and chilli plants still outside — will not shrug off a hard frost. Move pots to a porch, a greenhouse, or against a south-facing wall, and cover what you cannot move. See the crop pages for tomatoes, courgettes and basil.

Hardy plants already meant to sit through winter, such as garlic and autumn-sown broad beans, usually need no extra wrap in an ordinary frost. Wet soil and standing water do more harm than cold air. The longer note is garlic and broad beans over winter. What to sow in winter lists the jobs that still make sense in December, January and February.

Early potato shoots are easily blackened. Earth them up, or cover them if a late frost is forecast, as the early potatoes page says. Chitting belongs indoors, in a frost-free place; see how to chit seed potatoes.

Fleece, cloches and covers

A sheet of horticultural fleece, pegged so it does not blow onto the path, takes the edge off a light frost for a low row or a newly planted pot. Open or lift it on a mild day so the air can move. A cover left sealed in damp weather becomes a wet blanket and can rot the leaves it was meant to save.

Cloches and a cold frame do a similar job for a short row. Water the soil before a hard night only if it is dry; wet soil holds a little more heat than dry dust, but a waterlogged bed is a different problem and should not be soaked again.

Fleece is a shop item like any other. You can look at a Kingfisher horticultural fleece sheet (opens in a new tab) or Smart Garden fleece covers (opens in a new tab) made as bags for pots. Those are examples, not a ranking, and there are no prices on this page. Thompson & Morgan (opens in a new tab) and Crocus (opens in a new tab) also sell covers and plants. Bring a tender pot indoors if you can. Fleece is a help, not a promise.

In spring

Do not plant or harden off tender seedlings just because the calendar says May. Wait until nights are settling above freezing, and use hardening off rather than a sudden move from a warm room to an open bed. A late frost after a mild spell is ordinary UK weather. Keep fleece to hand until your own garden has had its last hard night.

Common questions

What is the first frost date for my area?

This site does not publish one. Look at a local forecast, and at the nights that have actually frosted in your garden. Coastal and urban gardens are often later in autumn than inland or high ones, but a frost pocket can sit in a mild region. A date copied from a table will not see the hollow at the bottom of your plot.

Will fleece save tomatoes outside in December?

No. Once the nights are regularly at or below freezing, a tender plant belongs under cover or in the compost. Fleece is for a short, light frost, or for hardening plants at the beginning and end of the season, not for keeping a summer crop outside all winter.
