Cleaning bird feeders

By Robin & Radish · 21 November 2026

Clean and move bird feeders every week, change the water every day, and avoid flat feeders such as bird tables, following the RSPB's current hygiene advice.

A feeder full of damp, clumped food does more harm than an empty one. Birds meet each other at feeders, and droppings, old food and dirty water spread disease. Cleaning is part of feeding, not an optional extra. The RSPB's current "Feed Seasonally, Feed Safely" guidance, dated 30 October 2026, is to clean feeders weekly, move them weekly, change the water every day, and choose hanging feeders over flat feeders such as bird tables. The food itself, and the months for seeds and peanuts, are covered in bird food for winter and feeding birds in spring and summer. The same routine covers feeders used by blue tits, goldfinches and starlings.

A simple routine

Once a week is the habit the RSPB now asks for. Do it sooner if the food is wet, clumped or mouldy, and check more often in warm, damp weather. In a hard frost the food stays drier, but the feeder still needs a look.

  1. Throw away food that is left. Do not mix it back into the fresh bag.
  2. Scrub the feeder with hot, soapy water and a brush you keep for this job.
  3. Disinfect it. The RSPB suggests a non-toxic disinfectant sold for this use, such as Ark-Klens, or a mild (5%) bleach solution, and then rinse until there is no smell and no slippery residue.
  4. Let it dry before you refill. A wet feeder turns the next fill into a lump.
  5. Put out a smaller amount if the last lot was not finished.
  6. Clean away old food and droppings from the ground underneath.

Move the feeder to a different spot every week so droppings do not build up on one patch of ground. If you have more than one feeder, spread them out so fewer birds pile onto the same perch. Do not hang a feeder under a tree or a roost, where droppings can fall on the food.

The RSPB advises against feeders with flat surfaces, such as bird tables, window feeders and trays, because contaminated food collects there and the risk of disease is higher. A hanging feeder that keeps the food dry is the better choice. Retire a wooden table that stays greasy and cannot be scrubbed clean. Plastic and metal come clean if you actually take them apart. Many tube feeders unscrew. The base is where the mess sits.

Only put water out if you can change it every day. The trichomonosis parasite can survive in water. Rinse and scrub the dish whenever it is dirty, and disinfect it weekly. A shallow dish is easier to clean than a deep one. In winter, break ice and wash the dish when it is slimy, not only when you remember. The RSPB prefers tap water in most places; check your water company's treatment if you want the detail.

Hands

Wash your hands when you finish. Wear gloves if you have cuts. Do not clean feeders in the kitchen sink and then wash up in the same water. A bucket outside is the right place. Some bird diseases can infect people. The risk is small, but it is worth taking those precautions.

If birds look unwell

A bird that is fluffed up, sluggish, and slow to fly from the feeder may be ill. One sleepy bird on a cold morning is not a diagnosis. Several birds looking like that, especially finches, is a reason to act.

The RSPB's advice is to stop feeding altogether, thoroughly clean the feeders and baths, and store them away from the garden, for example in a shed. Only start again after a month, and only if no more sick birds can be seen. That reduces the chance of birds passing an infection on at a crowded perch. This is general garden practice, not a veterinary examination. If you keep chickens or other captive birds, keep their food and water well away from wild-bird feeders.

Where the feeder hangs

Cleaning is easier if you can lift the feeder down without a ladder every time. Hang it where you can see the birds and see whether the food is fresh. Keep it off a fence a cat uses as a path, and off the ground under a roost. The robin guide covers birds that feed low, which still do better with a hanging mealworm or suet feeder than with a table.

Brushes and kit

A bottle brush or a bird feeder cleaning brush (opens in a new tab) helps you reach inside a tube. That Amazon link is a search, not a particular brush. Hot water and washing-up liquid do the work, before the disinfectant step. Replace a cracked feeder rather than leaving a seam that will not come clean. If you are buying a feeder, pick one you can take apart and that sheds rain, not a flat table.

Common questions

Can I just rinse the feeder?

A rinse leaves the film that holds bacteria. Scrub, disinfect, rinse again, and dry. If you are short of time, it is better to leave the feeder empty and dry than to refill a dirty one.

How much disinfectant?

Follow the instructions on the bottle for the product you bought, and rinse thoroughly afterwards. If you use a mild bleach solution, the RSPB's figure is 5%. More concentrate is not more useful if the birds then refuse the feeder or drink the residue in a bath. The drying step matters as much as the product.

Should I stop feeding in summer so I can skip cleaning?

From 1 May to 31 October, pause seeds and peanuts even if you like to keep a feeder up. Small amounts of mealworms, fat balls or suet can still go out, in a hanging feeder, and those still need a weekly clean and a weekly move. Warm wet weather spoils food faster than a frost does. Stopping only helps if the feeder is actually empty and clean, not half full of old seed.
