Saving vegetable seed

By Robin & Radish · 17 November 2026

Which UK garden vegetables are worth saving seed from, which ones will not come true, and how to dry and store a small packet.

Saving seed is worth doing for a few crops where the next generation looks like the plant you grew. It is a poor bet for anything sold as an F1 hybrid, and for vegetables that cross with their neighbours before you can see it.

This is home gardening, not a seed catalogue. Save a little, label it, and buy fresh seed when the crop matters more than the experiment. Parsnip seed in particular does not keep. Old parsnip seed is a common reason a row never comes up.

Worth saving

Peas and beans. Peas, broad beans, French beans and runner beans are the straightforward group if you grew a named open-pollinated variety, not an F1. Leave a few healthy pods on the plant until they are dry and rattling. Finish the drying indoors if the weather turns wet. Do not save seed from plants with spotted or mottled leaves. Bean diseases travel in the seed.

Tomatoes. An ordinary (not F1) tomato usually comes reasonably true, because the flower often pollinates itself. Scoop the seed onto a piece of kitchen paper, spread it, and let it dry. Label the paper with the variety. F1 varieties, which is a lot of the supermarket-style plants, will not repeat the fruit you liked.

Lettuce. Let one lettuce bolt and collect the seed when the heads turn fluffy, on a dry day. Lettuce will cross with other lettuce flowering nearby, so the next row may be a mix. That is fine for leaves. It is not a way to keep one named hearting variety pure.

Leave these alone at first

Carrots, parsnips, beetroot and onions are biennials. They flower in their second year, and they cross readily with other varieties and, for carrots, with wild relatives. Saving them is a project, not a first attempt.

Courgettes, pumpkins and squash cross with each other. Seed from this year's fruit can give a different plant next year. Eat the fruit. Buy the seed.

Do not save seed from a plant you grew because it was diseased, or from a plant that bolted at the first warm week if what you wanted was a long row of leaves. You would be keeping that habit.

Drying and keeping

Dry seed is the whole job. Pods that feel dry outside can still be damp in the middle after a wet August. Spread them on a plate indoors, out of the sun, until they snap rather than bend.

Put the seed in a paper envelope, not a sealed plastic bag. Write the crop and the year. Keep it cool and dry, not in a shed that drips. Use peas and beans the next spring. Tomato and lettuce seed lasts longer than parsnip seed, but a labelled year stops you sowing a forgotten packet.

The months you then sow that seed are the same as bought seed. Use the sowing planner.

Common questions

Can I save seed from a supermarket tomato?

You can dry the seed. If the variety was an F1, the plants you get will not match that fruit. If you do not know, treat it as an experiment and do not rely on it for the only row.

The bean pods went mouldy. Can I still use them?

No. Mould and spotted leaves are a reason to throw the seed away and buy a packet. Drying only works if the pod was healthy and you then kept it dry.

How long can I keep it?

Peas and beans are best the following year. A cool, dry envelope of tomato or lettuce seed is often still worth sowing after that, and you will see at germination if it is not. Parsnip seed is the one to buy fresh every year.
